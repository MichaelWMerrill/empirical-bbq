Two Thanksgivings, two birds, same 14-lb weight on the tag. One year I brined a whole turkey overnight. The next, I spatchcocked one dry and roasted it flat because I'd read it cooks faster and more evenly. Both came out good. What I didn't do, either year, was ask which one actually put more carved meat on the platter for the same raw pounds bought — I just assumed "better technique" meant "better yield." It doesn't, not automatically, and the two techniques don't even help for the same reason.

## Brining buys you less evaporation, full stop

Our yield model treats a turkey cook as two sequential losses: **trim** (giblets, neck, and for spatchcock the backbone) and **cook loss** (moisture and fat that leaves during the smoke). Brining only touches the second number. A salt brine draws water into the muscle before it ever sees heat, and that extra bound moisture is harder to evaporate out — so the model drops cook loss from **28% unbrined to 20% brined**, applied to whatever's left after trim. Nothing else about the bird changes: same trim fraction, same starting weight.

Run a 14-lb whole bird through both settings and the difference isn't subtle. At 5% trim you start with 13.3 lb of meat and bone. Unbrined, 28% cook loss leaves **9.58 lb** carved. Brined, 20% cook loss leaves **10.64 lb** — better than a pound more meat off the same bird, purely from resisting evaporation.

## Spatchcocking buys you speed, and costs you a little trim

Spatchcocking is a completely different lever. Cutting out the backbone doesn't change how much moisture the meat loses during the cook — the model keeps cook loss identical, 28%/20% brined-or-not, whether the bird is whole or flattened. What it changes is the **trim fraction**: removing the backbone bumps trim loss from **5% to 7%**, because that bone and cartilage is now leaving the bird before it ever goes on the grate instead of getting picked around at the carving board.

That's a small yield cost — on a 14-lb bird, the extra 2% trim is a bit over a quarter pound — in exchange for the real reason people spatchcock: laying the bird flat opens it up to heat from every direction, and the stall engine's rate modifier gives spatchcock a **1.35× speed multiplier** on top of the geometry effect that already makes turkey fast. That's a genuinely faster, more even cook. It's just not a yield play.

> **The two levers, side by side (14-lb whole bird, our model):**
> - Baseline (whole, unbrined): 13.3 lb trimmed → **9.58 lb carved**
> - Brined only: 13.3 lb trimmed → **10.64 lb carved** (+1.06 lb)
> - Spatchcocked only: 13.02 lb trimmed → **9.37 lb carved** (−0.21 lb, but ~35% faster climb)
> - Both: 13.02 lb trimmed → **10.42 lb carved** (net still up from baseline)

## Stack them and the brine wins the ledger

Combine both and you get 13.02 lb trimmed at 20% cook loss: **10.42 lb** carved — still comfortably ahead of the plain unbrined whole bird, because the brine's percentage-point swing on cook loss is worth roughly four times what the backbone costs you in trim. If your only goal is maximum carved weight per raw pound bought, brine is doing essentially all of the work; spatchcock is riding along for the cook-time win, not the yield one.

None of this makes spatchcocking a bad idea — a faster, more even cook is worth a quarter pound of backbone most years, especially with [turkey's two-zone doneness problem](/turkey-stall) (breast pulled at 160°F, thighs pushed toward 175°F) to manage on top of everything else. It just means "spatchcock for yield" isn't the right reason to reach for kitchen shears. Brine for yield. Spatchcock for time and evenness.
