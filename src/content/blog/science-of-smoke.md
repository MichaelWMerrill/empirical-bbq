Ran out of coarse 16-mesh pepper mid-rub once and finished the job with regular fine table-ground stuff, figuring pepper is pepper. The bark that came off that brisket was noticeably thinner and flakier than my usual — same rub recipe, same smoker, same wood, same everything else. Took some reading afterward to understand that bark isn't burnt seasoning sitting on top of the meat. It's a chemical reaction, and the rub's particle size was one of the inputs I hadn't been thinking about at all.

## Bark is a polymer, not a crust

Two separate reactions build bark. The **Maillard reaction** — amino acids and sugars browning under heat — usually peaks above 280°F, but the long, low-and-slow environment of a smoker triggers a slower, deeper version of the same browning over hours instead of minutes. The bigger driver is **polymerization**: the organic compounds carried in wood smoke dissolve into the surface fat and moisture on the meat and cross-link with the rub, curing into a dark, shiny, resilient skin. That skin is bark. It's not char, and it's not just dried-out spice — it's smoke compounds and rub bonded into a new surface layer.

## Coarse rub gives the reaction more to grab

That's where my fine-ground pepper went wrong. Coarse 16-mesh pepper and kosher salt sit on the surface as larger, more textured particles, and those particles act as physical anchor points — more surface area, more edges, more places for smoke compounds to land and cross-link into. Fine table-ground pepper is smoother and packs tighter, giving the same reaction less to hold onto, which is exactly why my substitute batch came out thinner. Binders like mustard or oil don't add flavor of their own; they change the surface tension and moisture the rub sits in, helping the whole layer dissolve and cure more evenly rather than in patches.

## The stall isn't the enemy of bark — early wrapping is

Bark-building shares its clock with [the stall](/blog/physics-of-the-stall) — the same evaporative-cooling plateau, typically landing in the mid-150s to 170°F depending on pit temp. That overlap is useful: while the surface sits there evaporating, it's also still curing. Wrap before the bark has actually set and you trap steam against a surface that hadn't finished the reaction yet. A rough test that still holds up: if you can scratch the rub off with a fingernail, it isn't done curing regardless of what the clock says.

Wrap material afterward decides how much of that bark survives. Peach butcher paper is porous — steam can still escape while fat stays put — so bark softens slightly but keeps its bite. Foil seals in a 100%-humidity braising environment, which is the fastest way through the stall and also the fastest way to turn a hard-earned bark soft.

## Bark survives the rest, if you let it vent first

One more spot bark gets undone after the smoker: straight off the pit and into a sealed cooler, trapped steam softens the crust just as effectively as foil did. Give the brisket a few minutes to vent down before it goes into the hold — the difference between bark that's still there at dinner and bark that dissolved back into the meat is often just that.

Wrap choice changes more than bark, too — it moves your cooked yield the same way grade or trim style does.
