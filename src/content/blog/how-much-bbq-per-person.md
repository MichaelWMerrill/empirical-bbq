Bought what felt like an absurd amount of pork butt for a 20-person cookout once — two full 8 lb bone-in butts, more than 1.5 lb of raw meat per guest — and still ran out with people still in line. I'd priced it off the *cooked* weight I wanted to serve, the same way I'd shop for a roast chicken dinner, and never accounted for how much of that raw weight simply isn't there anymore by the time it comes off the smoker.

Every cookout starts with the same anxious question: **how much meat do I buy?** Get it wrong and you either run out an hour in or eat leftovers for a week. The mistake most people make — the one I made — is planning around the *cooked* weight, when the number that matters at the butcher is the *raw* weight.

## Step 1: How Much Cooked Meat Per Guest

A reasonable main-course plate, with sides:

- **Brisket or turkey:** ~0.5 lb cooked per guest.
- **Pulled pork:** ~0.33 lb cooked per guest.

Scale it to your crowd — lighter for snackers, heavier if you want leftovers or you are feeding a hungry crew.

## Step 2: The Shrinkage Tax

Here is the part people forget, and the part that burned me. Low-and-slow cooking is brutal on weight, and it happens in two separate stages that both take a bite:

- **Trim** — fat cap, hard fat, silver skin, and (for a bone-in cut) bone and connective tissue that never becomes servable meat.
- **Cook loss** — rendering and evaporation over hours at the pit, which keeps taking weight long after trimming is done.

The size of each bite depends on the cut and how you cook it. A **choice-grade brisket**, commercially trimmed and wrapped in paper, loses about 24% to trim and another 34% of what's left to the cook — landing near **50% total yield**, half the raw weight you bought. **Pulled pork** is gentler: a bone-in butt loses about 12% to trim and 38% to the cook (~55% yield); go boneless and trim loss drops to 6%, pushing yield closer to **58%**. **Turkey** is the best deal of the three — trim is just giblets and neck, so a brined, whole bird can land as high as **~76% yield**, though an unbrined spatchcocked one dips closer to **67%** since spatchcocking removes the backbone and skips the moisture brining retains.

Wrap and brine choices move these numbers more than people expect. Foil traps steam and cuts cook loss the most (fastest, wettest cook); paper is a middle ground; cooking naked loses the most moisture of the three. None of that is a reason to always foil — it's a bark-versus-yield tradeoff, same as the one that shapes [when you wrap for the stall](/blog/wrap-timing-not-just-what) — but it's worth knowing it also changes how much dinner you get out of the same raw weight.

## Step 3: Raw Weight Isn't Cooked Weight ÷ 2

So the raw target is not the cooked target. It is:

> **raw weight = cooked needed ÷ yield fraction**

To serve **10 lb of cooked brisket**, at ~50% yield you buy closer to **20 lb of raw packer** — not 10, not 15. That's exactly where my pork math went wrong the other direction: I'd bought raw weight like it *was* the serving weight, so my "generous" 1.5 lb-per-guest purchase actually worked out to well under a pound of pulled pork per plate once trim and cook loss were done with it.

| Protein | Prep | Typical yield | Raw needed for 10 lb cooked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brisket | Choice, commercial trim, paper | ~50% | ~20 lb |
| Pulled pork | Bone-in, paper | ~55% | ~18 lb |
| Pulled pork | Boneless, paper | ~58% | ~17 lb |
| Turkey | Whole, brined | ~76% | ~13 lb |
| Turkey | Spatchcock, unbrined | ~67% | ~15 lb |
