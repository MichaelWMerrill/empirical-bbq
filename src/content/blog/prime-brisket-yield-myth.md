Splurged on a Prime packer for a big cook, figured the extra marbling meant extra everything — flavor, tenderness, and yes, more meat for the money. Sliced it up next to a Choice brisket I'd run a month earlier at the same raw weight, and the Prime came in visibly lighter on the cutting board. Same 14 lb on the scale at the butcher counter. Fewer slices at the table. I went back and ran both through the numbers, and the gap wasn't a fluke.

## The two-step loss

Every brisket loses weight twice before it reaches a plate: once to **trim** (fat cap and hard fat removed before it ever sees smoke) and once to **cook loss** (rendering and evaporation over the smoke). The model chains them:

> **cooked weight = raw weight × (1 − trim%) × (1 − cook%)**

Both fractions come out of an empirical loss matrix keyed by grade and, for cook loss, by wrap method — and that matrix is where the surprise lives.

## Grade sets the trim, and Prime loses that fight

USDA grade tracks marbling, and marbling comes with fat — including a thicker fat cap that has to come off in trim before the brisket goes on the smoker. At commercial trim, the matrix has:

| Grade | Trim loss | Cook loss (paper) | Total yield (14 lb raw) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prime | 28% | 35% | **46.8%** → 6.55 lb cooked |
| Choice | 24% | 34% | **50.2%** → 7.02 lb cooked |
| Select | 19% | 33% | **54.3%** → 7.6 lb cooked |

Cook loss barely moves between grades — a percentage point here or there — but trim loss swings a full 9 points from Select to Prime. That thicker fat cap that makes Prime taste richer is also fat you're paying premium-grade price for and then cutting off the cutting board before the meat ever hits smoke. Same 14 lb raw brisket, and Select puts over a pound more cooked meat on the platter than Prime does.

Run the actual dollars and the gap widens further. At the site's market defaults (Prime $5.49/lb, Choice $4.29/lb, Select $3.49/lb raw), Prime's sticker price is 57% higher than Select's. But because Prime also yields worse, the **true cost per cooked pound** comes out to $11.73 for Prime against $6.43 for Select — an 82% gap. You're not just paying more per pound of brisket; you're paying more per pound of *finished barbecue*, by a wider margin than the price tag alone suggests.

## Wrap moves the number almost as much as two grades

Grade isn't the only lever, and it isn't even the biggest one you control the day of the cook. Compare a Prime brisket cooked naked against one wrapped in foil, commercial trim held constant:

> **Naked: 43.2% yield · Foil: 49.7% yield — a 6.5-point swing from wrap choice alone**

That's almost as large as the **7.0-point swing** between a naked Prime and a naked Select at the same commercial trim — a full two grades apart. The wrap you choose for bark and moisture reasons is doing nearly as much work on your final yield as the grade you paid extra for at the butcher counter.

Trim style compounds in the same direction. Aggressive competition trim on a Prime brisket (42% trim, squared and defatted) against commercial trim (28%) costs another **9.7 points of yield** even with the same foil wrap — the price of that clean, contest-ready presentation.

## Run your own cut before you buy

None of this makes Prime the wrong choice — the marbling and flavor are real, and yield isn't the only thing on the menu. But if you're planning a guest count against a raw-weight budget, grade and wrap both move the number more than most shoppers assume, and in opposite directions from what the price tag implies.
