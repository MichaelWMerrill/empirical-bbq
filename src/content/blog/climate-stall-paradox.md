Smoked the same brisket twice, three weeks apart — same rub, same offset, same 225°F setpoint. First cook was on the Gulf coast in July, something like 85% humidity, air you could wring out. The second was at altitude out west, air so dry it felt like it was pulling the moisture straight out of my sinuses. I expected the dry cook to just run hotter and faster across the board — less resistance, more of everything. Instead the dry brisket hit its stall *sooner* by internal temperature than the humid one, then sat there noticeably longer before it broke. Once I wrapped it, it caught up fast — faster than any cook I'd run before. The humid brisket did the reverse on every count: held off stalling until a higher temperature, blew through the plateau in a hurry once it started, then climbed at an entirely ordinary pace after wrapping. Same meat, same pit, same method — three numbers, each one moving in its own direction depending on what the sky was doing that day.

## Three dials, not one

The site's stall model treats regional climate as three independent multipliers layered on top of the base cook — not one "dry vs. humid" toggle, but three separate knobs that don't all turn the same way:

> **arid:** stall onset ×0.95 (earlier) · stall duration ×1.1 (longer) · post-wrap climb ×1.2 (faster)
> **humid:** stall onset ×1.05 (later) · stall duration ×0.9 (shorter) · post-wrap climb ×1.0 (unchanged)

At a 225°F pit, brisket's baseline stall threshold sits at 155°F. Multiply that by the arid and humid factors and the plateau's *starting point* alone swings from roughly **147°F** on a bone-dry day to roughly **163°F** on a humid one — a 16-degree difference in exactly where the flat spot on your thermometer graph begins, before duration or finish speed even enter the picture.

## Why dry air starts the stall earlier

The stall itself is evaporative cooling: moisture leaving the meat's surface pulls away exactly as much energy as the pit is putting in, so the internal temperature parks until the surface runs out of easy water. What drives that evaporation is the **vapor-pressure deficit** — the gap between how much moisture the surrounding air can still absorb and how much is sitting on the meat's surface. Dry air has a much bigger gap, so it pulls moisture off the surface more aggressively, at a lower internal temperature. That's the earlier onset. Humid air has a much smaller gap, so evaporation can't out-pace the incoming heat until the meat is already hotter — a later onset.

## Then it reverses for duration

The same dry air that starts pulling moisture sooner also has more total capacity left to keep pulling it, so once the plateau begins, it runs about 10% longer than the baseline. Humid air, having waited longer to start, doesn't have much of a vapor-pressure gap left to sustain a long pull — so once it does stall, it resolves about 10% faster. Onset and duration move in opposite directions from the same underlying gap, which is the part that reads as backwards until you trace it back to the physics.

## And a third twist once you wrap

Wrapping cuts off evaporation and switches the cook to a straight thermal-climb problem. Here arid air keeps one more edge: a parched, already-dried surface conducts the remaining heat in faster, so the model gives arid cooks a 20% quicker post-wrap climb to the finish. Humid air gets no such boost — its post-wrap climb runs at the same rate as the moderate baseline.

Every one of these is deliberately kept modest — the model documents them as ±5–10% estimates, symmetric around a neutral "moderate" baseline, not a dominant variable like your wrap choice or the meat's mass. They won't reorder your afternoon. But they're exactly why the same brisket recipe seems to behave a little differently depending on where you're standing when you run it, and why the stall you learned to expect at home doesn't quite repeat on the road.
